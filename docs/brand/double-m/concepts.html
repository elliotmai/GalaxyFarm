Double M Brand Sheet

Six marks, kept from twenty-seven. Numbering is the original sheet’s, so the gaps are the cuts. Every mark is drawn in livestock-brand grammar, coloured from the locked palette in packages/config/tailwind.preset.ts, and shown on both real grounds — theme is fixed per surface, so a mark proven on midnight is not thereby proven on linen.

Each carries two verdicts. Screen is the pixel test: rasterised at true 24 and 16 px, because SVG draws crisply at any size and flatters everything. Iron is the blotch test: every stroke thickened 1.7× to stand in for heat spreading through hide. They are the same question asked twice — both destroy the narrow gap between two roughly-parallel strokes first — but they are not the same threshold, and two of these six pass one and fail the other.

The six

Two are drawn in the rounded brand-iron language of the original sheet; four keep the sharper language of your sketches — mitred corners, flat stroke ends. That split is the real decision here, and the hot-iron row below has something to say about it.

Rocking Double M

Holds at 16px Iron: fails
Says
Straight translation of the mark already approved: same rocker, same shared ground, letters where the stars were. The least disruptive thing you could put next to the star mark.
Screen
The clearest “M M” of the six. Two whole letters with a real gap between them, and the gap survives 24 px and 16 px intact.
Iron
Fails, and this is new — nobody had asked it of this mark before. Under a 1.7× spread the V inside each M closes from the valley upward and survives only as a nick at the top, and the rocker crowds the letters above it. See the hot-iron row.

Rocking Connected Double M

Holds at 16px Iron: fails
Says
The zigzag with a stem dropped from the shared apex, so each M gets its inner leg back. Joined into one glyph and still reading as two letters — which the plain connected version does not manage.
Screen
Holds at 24 px and 16 px. The stem is what does it: without it the mark reads as a crown.
Iron
Fails, and tighter than the mark above: the stem lands 18 units from each valley, which leaves 4 units of clear space once the stroke has spread. It is the closest pair of strokes in either rounded mark.

Angular, tight

Tight at 16px Iron: passes
Says
Two all-diagonal M’s, the second smaller and dropped. The asymmetry does the dam-and-calf job the star mark does, without needing a second size of anything drawn in.
Screen
Reads at 24 px. At 16 the smaller M softens first — the 6-unit line is lighter than everything else here and it greys at exactly the size the nav bar uses.
Iron
Passes, despite being the busiest drawing of the six. Nothing in it runs parallel and close, so there is no channel for heat to close.
Pairs with
Number 27 below is this same drawing opened to iron tolerances. Keeping both is the comparison: this is the sketch as drawn, that is the sketch made buildable.

Overlapped, opened

Holds at 16px Iron: passes
Says
Two whole M’s, half-overlapped, four legs down. The most literally a double M of anything here — nothing shared, nothing abbreviated.
Built to
All four legs evenly spaced at 26 units, which is nearly three times the stroke. The two V’s clear each other by 11 units where in the original sketch they ran nearly parallel.
Note
Each M’s valley lands exactly on its neighbour’s leg. That is a weld rather than a collision — an iron makes a T-junction happily — and it is what turns the pair from two letters that overlap into one interlocked object.

Double-struck, vertical

Holds at 16px Iron: passes
Says
One M struck twice, the second strike below the first. It says “double” by repeating the whole letter rather than by standing two letters side by side — the only one of the six that does.
Built to
The legs coincide and reinforce into single strokes; the two V’s stack 28 units apart, a 12-unit clearance where the original sketch had none.
Why vertical
Arithmetic, not taste. A sideways offset has to reach 18 units to clear the legs, but short of exactly half the letter width the first M’s valley lands within 4 units of the second M’s leg — and at exactly half you have number 25 above. There is no horizontal double-strike that is neither blotchy nor already Overlapped.

Angular, opened

Holds at 16px Iron: passes
Says
Number 24 with the gaps opened: valleys widened to 72°, the small M widened to 22 units across its apexes, and its outer stroke laid down shallower.
Built to
The crossing now meets at 45° instead of 34°. At 45° it burns as a crossing; at 34° it burns as a blot.
Note
It needed the least work of the three refinements and it still survives smallest. If the sharp language wins, this is the one to build the family around.

The pixel test

All six rasterised at true 24 px and 16 px, then magnified — the actual pixels a nav bar and a favicon would get.

The hot-iron test

Each mark as drawn, then with every stroke thickened 1.7× — standing in for heat spreading into the gaps as the iron meets hide. If a gap is still open on the right, heat will not close it.

The split this exposes

The four sharp marks pass the iron. The two rounded ones fail it. Not dramatically — they do not vanish. Under the spread the V inside each M closes from the valley upward and survives only as a nick at the very top, and the rocker crowds the letters sitting on it. Measured rather than eyeballed: at 1.7× the V in number 1 has about 14 units of clear space at the apexes and none by half its depth, against a rule that wants at least one stroke width the whole way.

The cause is proportion, not language. Numbers 1 and 11 pack two whole M’s into the same box the sharp marks give to one-and-a-bit, so each letter is about half the width while the stroke stays the same — roughly 29% of each letterform is ink, against 17% for number 25. They were drawn to a 24 px legibility standard, which they meet comfortably, and never to an iron standard, which nobody had asked of them until now.

That is fixable the same way 25–27 were fixed — widen the letters, or drop to one M and a rocker — but it is a redraw, not a tweak, and a wider mark sits differently next to the approved star mark than these do now. Worth deciding whether a physical brand is actually in scope before spending that. If it is only ever a screen mark, 1 and 11 are the two clearest “M M”s on the sheet and the iron column does not matter.

Where that leaves it

Nothing here is approved; this is still exploration against issue #26. But the six now sort cleanly by what they are for.

If the mark has to be burnable

Pick from 25, 26 and 27. All three were drawn to iron tolerances and all three hold at 16 px, so they carry no compromise on the screen side either. 27 survives smallest and needed the least work, which makes it the natural centre of a family; 25 is the most literally a double M if that reading matters more than economy.

If it is a screen mark only, 1 and 11 are the clearest pair of letters here and keep the approved rocker, which no other survivor does.

24 and 27 are the same drawing at two weights and are worth deciding together rather than separately — 24 is your sketch as drawn, 27 is it made buildable.

One thing to settle whichever wins

The two shipped files disagree about colour roles. rocking-double-star-customer.svg draws the stars in identity purple with the rocker in bluebonnet blue, while logomark.tsx and the admin file draw the stars in --gf-text with the rocker in --gf-identity — so the component and the customer SVG render different marks on a light ground, and the brand README’s own description matches neither exactly.

These follow the component: letters take the text colour on midnight and identity purple on linen; the supporting element takes the second colour. Worth deciding deliberately rather than inheriting.
