Brand concepts · drawn as irons · issue #26

Double M Brand Sheet

Eight marks, every one of them a thing you could actually have made. A branding iron is bent steel bar — so every corner on this sheet is an arc, because flat stock bends and does not fold. A mitred corner is not a drawing decision, it is a cut and two welds.

That inverts what the earlier sheets assumed. They treated the sharp, mitred language as the buildable one and the rounded language as decorative. It is the other way round: the rounded corners were the honest ones all along, and the sharp set could not have been made without cutting every corner and welding it back.

Screen behaviour is deliberately secondary here and is noted only where it bites. These are drawn to be burned first.

What an iron can do

Five constraints, all of them physical. Everything on this sheet is checked against them by measurement rather than eye, and the numbers are quoted per mark.

RuleValueBecause
Bar face6.5% of letter height A ¼ inch bar on a 4 inch brand. This is the single number the earlier sheets had wrong — they drew at 16%, which is a bar so fat the minimum bend swallows the letter.
Bend radius1.5 × bar width Flat bar will not take a tighter radius without cracking. Below this you are cutting and welding, not bending.
Minimum angle60° An acute crotch pools heat from both arms and burns through as a solid blot.
Clear space2.5 × bar width The scar spreads to about 1.7× the bar face. Two strokes closer than this merge into one.
Piecesfewer is better Disconnected elements need a backing plate, and each piece heats at its own rate. One bent length is the cheapest and most even iron you can have.

What those rules do to the letter M

An M’s apex is where a leg meets a diagonal, and its angle is leg splay + half the valley angle. Both that apex and the valley have to clear 60°, and the arithmetic is unforgiving: a brandable M has no vertical strokes at all. Upright legs give a 32° apex. The legs have to splay.

Splaying them makes the letter wide — between 1.3 and 1.7 times as wide as it is tall, depending on how the angle budget is spent. The cheapest arrangement is a 90° valley with the legs splayed only 15°, which is what everything here uses. Two of those side by side cannot fit a square box, which is why the aspect ratios on this sheet run from 1.30:1 to 2.68:1 and why none of these is a favicon.

The stem in the old number 11 is simply not makeable. Dropping a bar from the shared apex splits that apex into two 30° crotches, and no size or weight fixes it. Dropping the apex instead separates the two M’s with no extra stroke and no crotch at all.

The hot-iron test

Each mark as drawn, then with every stroke thickened 1.7× — heat spreading into the gaps as the iron meets hide. Every gap that is open on the right is a gap that survives the burn.

What this cost

The two languages turned out to be one

The earlier sheets were organised around a fork — rounded brand-iron language against the sketches’ sharp mitred language — and treated it as the real decision. Once corners have to be bends, that fork closes. There is one language, and it is the rounded one. The sharp marks were not a different school of drawing; they were drawings of an iron nobody would make.

Two marks collapsed into each other on the way. Number 1b and number 25 resolve to identical geometry once you space them at the tightest overlap the clear-space rule allows, so they are one mark now. Nine went in, eight came out.

What the rules took away

The dam-and-calf contrast. The calf in number 24 can only be 86% of the dam — the bar does not shrink with the letter, so a smaller M runs out of clear space before it looks meaningfully smaller. The dramatic size difference in the original sketch is not available at any scale.

The square. Nothing here is close to 1:1 except number 26 at 1.30:1, and that only because it stacks the second strike instead of standing it alongside. If a square icon is a hard requirement, it is a requirement to not put two letters side by side.

The stem, as above — and with it the cleanest “two letters, not a crown” reading the old sheet had. The dropped apex does the same work, but more quietly.

Screen, briefly

Held over deliberately, but worth flagging: these are wide, so a 16 px square slot will letterbox them to about 8 px of letter. None of these is a favicon. A brand that burns and a mark that survives a browser tab are close to opposite problems, and this sheet has now been solved hard for the first. If both are needed the answer is probably two marks — the iron for physical use and signage, a squarer derivative for the interface — rather than one mark asked to do both.
