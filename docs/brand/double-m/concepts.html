Brand concepts · drawn as running brands · issue #26

Double M Brand Sheet

Nine marks, drawn as registrable livestock brands rather than as logos that gesture at one. Rules are taken from the Texas & Southwestern Cattle Raisers Association’s brand-design guidance and ordinary iron-making practice, and every mark is checked against them by measurement.

The corners took four goes. Mitred, which no bender will make. Then dodged entirely by splaying the legs until the M’s barely read as letters. Then drawn broken, on the strength of the ¼ inch notch the trade files at a corner. Then attached but not actually round — the outside edge curved while the inside collapsed to a cusp, which is a point wearing a curve. A corner has to be round on both edges, and rounded angles have their own name in the grammar: a letter drawn that way is running.

The notch is real, but it is a groove cut in the face of the iron so heat can escape a corner. It is not a gap in the design and does not belong in the artwork. So these have upright legs, ordinary proportions, and every corner attached and rounded.

The rules

Expressed against a letter height of 100, so a 4 inch character puts 1 inch at 25 units and ¼ inch at 6.25. iron-check.py in this directory verifies every mark and generates the path data used here.

RuleValueBecause
Reading orderleft to right, top to bottom, outside in Joined characters are read “connected”. It is why the bar is named before the letters in Bar Double M.
Units2 or more, rarely over 3 Simple brands are easier to read and less painful for the animal.
Cornersattached, rounded The bar runs through and turns through an arc. Rounded angles are called running in the grammar. A round has to be round on the inside edge too: centreline radius one bar width, so the inner edge is a real arc of half a bar rather than a cusp.
Straight run22% of every arm What is left of an arm once both its corners have taken their tangent. Below this the arcs meet, the peaks flatten, and the letter reads as an arch.
Notch¼ inch, in the face Filed at every sharp corner and every joint so heat can escape instead of burning through as a blot. A groove in the face of the iron — not a gap in the design, and not drawn here.
Bar face¼ inch 7% of letter height, the thin end of the range. Not a free choice: the minimum corner radius scales with the bar while the letter does not, so a fatter bar cannot turn a round corner without eating the letterform.
Character4×3 inch For calves under a year; 6×3½ for grown cattle. Ordinary letter proportions, taller than wide.
Clear space1 inch Between characters, and between any two parallel lines. Closer than that and they burn together as one mark.

What that fixed, and what it killed

What the bar width decides. Everything, as it turns out. The minimum corner radius scales with the bar; the letter does not. An earlier pass took the face from 7% of letter height to 10% because thin strokes “looked light” — an aesthetic call, made in passing, that quietly made a round corner impossible. At 10% the only radius that fits is smaller than half the bar, which is a cusp; widen the radius enough to be genuinely round and it removes a quarter of the letter’s height at the apex. At 7% all three fit at once: round inside and out, peak intact, valley deep enough to read.

The apex is 45° rather than the 32° an M naturally wants, for the same reason — the more acute the corner, the more of the peak a round removes. And the letters are wider than the 4×3 the guidance gives for a character, because the bar needs somewhere to turn.

Killed: Overlapped Double M and Double-struck Double M. Both are defined by putting strokes close together — that is the whole idea in each case — and the 1 inch minimum between parallel lines rules out both. No amount of redrawing saves them; they are not brands.

Also gone: the “angular” pair. Those existed only to dodge sharp corners by making every stroke a diagonal. With notching that problem does not exist, and they collapse back into an ordinary M.

Back from the dead: the joined middle leg. The earlier sheet concluded a stem was impossible because it made two 30° crotches. It does — and a notch at that joint is the standard answer to it.

The hot-iron test

Each mark as drawn, then with every stroke thickened 1.7× — the scar spreading as the iron meets hide. The notches close up under the spread, which is the point: the corner reads clean instead of burning through.

Where they sort

Connected Double M is the cheapest iron and the only single-unit mark — one zigzag of bar and a welded spur. Lazy Double M is the only one taller than wide, so it is the one to look at if a squarish lockup matters. Bar Double M buys the tying-together that the rocker gives for a modifier with no bends in it. Rocking Double M stays closest to the approved star mark and costs the most iron.

Screen, still held over

The picture improved a lot. These sit between 0.63:1 and 1.59:1, and now that the corners are attached there are no sub-pixel gaps to lose — the same artwork should carry to screen without needing a separate derivative, which was not true of either previous set. Lazy Double M at 0.63:1 is the only one that would sit comfortably in a square favicon slot; the rest will letterbox.
