Double M Brand Sheet

Nine marks, drawn as registrable livestock brands rather than as logos that gesture at one. Rules are taken from the Texas & Southwestern Cattle Raisers Association’s brand-design guidance and ordinary iron-making practice, and every mark is checked against them by measurement.

The thing the earlier sheets got wrong was the corners. They treated a sharp corner as a shape problem and reshaped the letters to avoid it — splaying the legs until the M’s were 1.6 times wider than tall and barely read as letters. That is not how the trade solves it. A sharp corner is a heat trap, and the fix is a ¼ inch notch filed into the face at the corner so the heat vents. The letter keeps its normal shape. M is named explicitly in the guidance as a letter that needs this.

So these have upright legs and ordinary proportions, and they carry visible breaks at every sharp corner and every joint — which is exactly what real brands look like up close.

The rules

Expressed against a letter height of 100, so a 4 inch character puts 1 inch at 25 units and ¼ inch at 6.25. iron-check.py in this directory verifies every mark and generates the path data used here.

RuleValueBecause
Reading orderleft to right, top to bottom, outside in Joined characters are read “connected”. It is why the bar is named before the letters in Bar Double M.
Units2 or more, rarely over 3 Simple brands are easier to read and less painful for the animal.
Notch¼ inch Cut at every sharp corner and wherever bars join or intersect. Heat pools in a corner and burns through as a blot; the notch lets it escape. Cut into the face only — the bar behind stays continuous.
Bar face¼ to ½ inch Edges slightly rounded. This sheet uses 10% of letter height — a ⅕ inch face on a 4 inch character.
Character4×3 inch For calves under a year; 6×3½ for grown cattle. Ordinary letter proportions, taller than wide.
Clear space1 inch Between characters, and between any two parallel lines. Closer than that and they burn together as one mark.

What that fixed, and what it killed

Fixed: the letterforms. Once the notch does the work, an M can have upright legs and a pointed apex like any other M. Aspect ratios drop from 1.30–2.68:1 to 0.62–1.61:1, which is simply what letters look like. The earlier sheet’s claim that “a brandable M has no vertical strokes” was an artefact of a rule I invented rather than looked up.

Killed: Overlapped Double M and Double-struck Double M. Both are defined by putting strokes close together — that is the whole idea in each case — and the 1 inch minimum between parallel lines rules out both. No amount of redrawing saves them; they are not brands.

Also gone: the “angular” pair. Those existed only to dodge sharp corners by making every stroke a diagonal. With notching that problem does not exist, and they collapse back into an ordinary M.

Back from the dead: the joined middle leg. The earlier sheet concluded a stem was impossible because it made two 30° crotches. It does — and a notch at that joint is the standard answer to it.

The nine

Named in brand grammar, modifier first. Each shown on both real grounds, with its unit count, notch count and aspect ratio.

Rocking Double M

3 units 6 notches 1.31:1
Reads
Modifier first, letter last: rocking, then double M. Three units — two letters and the rocker — which is as many as a brand should carry.
Iron
Rocker sits an inch clear of the letters, so it is a separate element on the backing rather than a weld.
Note
The closest thing here to the approved Rocking Double Star: same modifier, letters where the stars were.

The hot-iron test

Each mark as drawn, then with every stroke thickened 1.7× — the scar spreading as the iron meets hide. The notches close up under the spread, which is the point: the corner reads clean instead of burning through.

Where they sort

Connected Double M is the cheapest iron and the only single-unit mark — one zigzag of bar and a welded spur. Lazy Double M is the only one taller than wide, so it is the one to look at if a squarish lockup matters. Bar Double M buys the tying-together that the rocker gives for a modifier with no bends in it. Rocking Double M stays closest to the approved star mark and costs the most iron.

Screen, still held over

These are much closer to square than the last set, so the picture is less bleak, but the notches are the new question: a ¼ inch break is 6% of letter height, which at 16 px is a fraction of a pixel and will simply vanish. That is not a fault — a screen mark has no heat to vent — but it means the burned brand and the drawn logo are not the same artwork, and a screen version should probably close the notches up.
