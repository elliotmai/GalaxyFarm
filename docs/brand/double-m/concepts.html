Brand concepts · issue #26

Double M Brand Sheet

Twenty-four ways to burn two M’s, drawn in the same livestock-brand grammar as the approved Rocking Double Star and coloured from the locked palette in packages/config/tailwind.preset.ts. Nothing here replaces the star mark — these are candidates for the day the farm and business names land.

Every mark is shown on both real grounds, because theme is fixed per surface: Midnight Nebula for /admin and /kiosk, Bluebonnet Linen for /account, /book, /sitter and public pages. The verdict chip answers the one question the brand README already put on the record — does it still hold together at 24 px? The pixel test near the end answers the harder one, which is whether it still reads as two letters down there.

Every mark has a checkbox. Tick the ones worth keeping and the counter in the corner will build the list; Recommended fills in a starting shortlist you can edit. Numbers 17, 18 and 24 appear twice — once as a sketched base and again close up — and are the same design, so ticking either ticks both.

  • Rocking — set on a curve
  • Bar — over or under a line
  • Connected — sharing a stroke
  • Lazy — lying on its side
  • Crazy — turned upside down
  • Circle / Box / Diamond — enclosed

Take two: the Rocking Double M, connected

The first two entries each give something up. Rocking keeps both M’s whole but leaves a gap that closes at small sizes; Connected closes the gap by having the M’s share their middle apex, which costs each one its inner leg — that is why it reads as a mountain range rather than two letters. Keeping the vertical stem between them fixes exactly that: the M’s stay joined, and each one gets its inner leg back. Three depths for that stem.

Rocking Connected Double M

Holds at 24px
Says
The stem runs to the same baseline as the outer legs, so both M’s are complete and identical and the join is a shared stroke rather than a compromise. This is the one that reads “M M” instead of “zigzag”.
Watch
Three legs at one depth set up a strong vertical rhythm, and the rocker underneath now has to hold a busier mark. Check that the outer legs do not start reading as a box.

The pixel test

Everything above is SVG, which the browser draws crisply at any size and which therefore flatters every candidate. This is the same six marks rasterised at true 24 px and true 16 px and then magnified, so what you are looking at is the actual pixels a nav bar and a favicon would get.

Take three: a bar under the constellation, and a crossed pair

Two separate lines of enquiry. The first gives the constellation the same straight bar that rescued the solid zigzag, to see whether a heavy ground element buys the star chart any survival at small sizes. The second drops the shared apex altogether: two whole M’s, splayed and overlapped so their inner legs cross — the way two longhorns standing side by side overlap. Straight lines throughout, no curves.

Constellation Bar Double M

Tight at 24px
Says
The star chart given a floor. The bar is the one element heavy enough to hold its own at any size, so the mark keeps an anchor even once the stars themselves have gone to dust.
Watch
It buys presence, not legibility. At 24 px what is left is a purple bar with grey speckle above it — better than the bare constellation, which leaves nothing at all, but still not a nav-bar mark.
Use
Where it earns its keep is the middle range. From about 40 px the bar gives the constellation the weight it was missing, which makes it usable on a PDF header or an email signature, not just a splash screen.

Crossed Double M

Tight at 24px
Says
Two complete M’s, legs splayed, overlapped until the inner two cross. Nothing is shared and nothing is abbreviated — they are simply standing close enough to overlap, which is exactly what the reference photograph shows two longhorns doing.
Watch
The crossing is a real junction: the strokes merge where they meet rather than passing over and under, because an interlace would need a knockout in the ground colour and these marks are transparent by design.
Note
The second M sits six units lower. That offset is doing the work — it reads as two animals at slightly different depths rather than as one flat pattern, and it is what keeps the crossing from looking like a drafting error.

Take four: your eight bases

Straight from the sketch sheet, vectorised and regularised — same forms, tidied proportions, legs brought back to a normal M. These are drawn in the sketch’s own language rather than the sheet’s: sharp mitred corners, flat stroke ends, and a lighter 6-unit line, where everything above is rounded and heavy. That is a real fork. If one of these wins, the question stops being which mark and becomes whether the whole system goes sharp.

Overlapped

Two whole M’s, half-overlapped, four legs down. The most literal “double” here — and the narrow channel between the two inner legs is the first thing to fill in as it shrinks.

Double-struck

One M printed twice, a few units apart. It reads as weight and shadow rather than as two letters — it says “double” without ever saying “M M”.

Shallow middle

Two M’s meeting at a raised joint instead of a shared apex. The shallow notch is the whole idea, and it is the first casualty: by 24 px it has flattened into a plain zigzag.

Splayed crown

Three peaks with the middle one dropped, legs kicked out. Lowering the shared apex is what separates the two M’s — the same job the vertical stem did, done with geometry instead of an added stroke.

Wide crown

The same dropped-apex move on vertical legs. Squarer, steadier, and the cleanest of the eight when it gets small.

Nested V

One M with a second V hung inside it. The “double” lives in the repeated valley rather than in a repeated letter — the only base here that is not two M’s.

Angular, wide

Two all-diagonal M’s overlapped, not a vertical anywhere. Reads as range and horizon before it reads as letters.

Angular, tight

Same, with the second M smaller and dropped. The asymmetry does the dam-and-calf job the star mark does, without needing a second size of anything.

The eight at 40 and 24 px

Compared inside their own section rather than in the main bench below, because a 6-unit mitred line and a 9-unit rounded one do not lose their legibility at the same rate — putting them in one row would flatter the heavier drawing for reasons that have nothing to do with the shapes.

Overlapped

Tight at 24px

Double-struck

Fails at 24px
Says
One M struck twice a few units apart. It is the only idea here that says “double” through repetition of the whole letter rather than through two letters standing side by side, and at 96 px that reads as deliberate, like a printing offset.

The pixel test, a third time

Take five: the same three, made brandable

The two requirements turn out to be one requirement. A hot iron and a small raster destroy the same thing first — the narrow gap between two roughly-parallel strokes. Heat spreads into it and closes it; a 24 px grid has no pixel to spare for it. So a single set of rules serves both, and the marks below were drawn to those rules rather than tidied until they looked right.

  • Stroke 9 units, and no two roughly-parallel strokes closer than twice that, centre to centre.
  • Vertices may be acute; channels may not be narrow. A sharp point blunts under the iron and survives as a point. A long thin gap fills and never comes back.
  • Flat ends, mitred corners. A brand iron is cut and bent bar, so the drawing should look like bar.
  • Tested, not eyeballed: every stroke thickened to 15 — a 1.7× spread — to stand in for the burn, and rasterised at 24 and 16 px.

Overlapped, opened

Holds at 16px
Was
Four legs, but the two inner ones sat closer to each other than any other pair in the mark. That single channel set the floor: grey at 24 px, closed at 16.
Now
The overlap is opened to exactly half the letter, which spaces all four legs evenly at 26 units. The two V’s now clear each other by 11 units where they ran nearly parallel before.
Cost
Each M’s valley now lands exactly on its neighbour’s leg. That is a weld rather than a collision — an iron makes a T-junction happily — and it is what turns the pair from two letters that happen to overlap into one interlocked object. If that reads as too connected, the alternative is a wider mark, not a tighter one.

Double-struck, vertical

Holds at 16px
Was
Every stroke in the mark had a twin a few units away. The worst possible case: not one narrow channel but a mark made entirely of them.
Now
The second strike moved from beside the first to below it. The legs coincide and reinforce into single strokes, and the two V’s stack 28 units apart — a 12-unit clearance where before there was none.
Why not sideways
Arithmetic, not taste. The offset has to reach 18 units to clear the legs, but at anything short of exactly half the letter width the first M’s valley lands within 4 units of the second M’s leg. At exactly half, the legs space evenly and you have Overlapped. There is no horizontal double-strike that is neither blotchy nor already the mark above it.
Note
This lands on the same construction as base six, arrived at from the opposite direction — which is a decent sign it is the right resting place for the idea rather than a dodge.

Angular, opened

Holds at 16px
Was
The small M’s left apex passed within about 4 units of the big M’s right descent, and its own V was only 14 units across the apexes — both closed under weight.
Now
Valleys opened to 72°, the small M widened to 22 units across the apexes, and its outer stroke laid down shallower so the crossing meets at 45° instead of 34°. A crossing at 45° burns as a crossing; at 34° it burns as a blot.
Note
It needed the least work of the three and it is still the one that survives smallest. Nothing in it runs parallel and close, which was true of the sketch and is the property worth protecting if it gets developed further.

If you want a recommendation

Not one mark — a family of three. This app has a 24 px nav, a 512 px maskable icon and a PDF that gets signed, and no single drawing is good at all three. The primary changed after the pixel test: Bar Double M held it until the raster showed it reading as a crown rather than as two letters at small sizes.

This picks from the rounded marks only. The eight sketched bases are a different drawing language, and choosing between the two is the prior question — if the answer is sharp, then Wide crown is the one to build the family around, and the three below get redrawn rather than reconsidered.

Primary

Rocking Connected Double M, posted

One connected object, so it cannot drift apart at any size, and the only candidate that still reads as two letters at 16 px. Keeps the approved rocker. Take the full-stem version instead if the stem landing in the rocker feels like one idea too many — it is the same mark, one decision quieter.

Square lockup

Stacked Double M

For the PWA and Apple touch icons, where the primary’s 2:1 proportion would have to be letterboxed into a square and lose half its presence. Not the favicon — at 16 px the primary still wins.

Hero & documents

Constellation Double M

For the kiosk splash, the login screen and the top of a PDF — anywhere it renders at 64 px or more on midnight, where it ties the logo to the pedigree screens.

One thing to settle first, whichever mark wins

The two shipped files disagree about colour roles, so any new mark inherits an unresolved question. rocking-double-star-customer.svg draws the stars in identity purple with the rocker in bluebonnet blue, while logomark.tsx draws the stars in --gf-text with the rocker in --gf-identity — which is what the admin file does. The component and the customer SVG therefore render different marks on a light ground.

These concepts follow the component: the letters take the text colour on midnight and identity purple on linen, and the supporting element — rocker, bar, ring, star — takes the second colour. Worth deciding deliberately rather than inheriting, since the README’s own description matches neither file exactly.
