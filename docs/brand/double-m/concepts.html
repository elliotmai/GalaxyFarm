Double M Brand Sheet

Nine candidates, named and drawn the way registered livestock brands are. The grammar is from TSCRA’s How to Design a Brand, this time read from the page itself rather than assembled from search summaries of it.

The letters are plain and sharp. That is how the sample brands are drawn — a regular A is a regular A, with a point on top. Earlier versions of this sheet mitred the corners, then splayed the letters to avoid corners, then broke the corners open, then rounded them off. All four were wrong, and the last two were wrong on the strength of iron-making guidance that has nothing to do with how a brand is drawn.

Two words in particular were being used backwards here. Running does not mean rounded angles; it means a cursive letter, denoted by curves. And rocking is not a curve placed under a letter — it is a curve attached to it. A curve that merely sits underneath is a different brand with a different name.

The grammar

What the page actually specifies. It is a vocabulary for naming and reading brands, plus one rule about simplicity — not a manufacturing standard.

RuleValueDetail
Keep it simple2–3 units “A brand design consists of two or more symbols. Many brands have three units. Few brands have more than three.” Simpler brands read better and are less painful for the animal.
Reading orderleft to right, top to bottom, outside in Joined letters take “connected” in the reading. Sometimes joined symbols are still read as though separated by a space.
Marksfour kinds Letters, numbers, lines and circles, pictures — alone or in combination.
Rockingattached below A curved mark attached to the bottom of a letter. A letter merely standing above a quarter circle is read “letter quarter circle” instead.
Swingingattached above A quarter or half circle attached to the top of a letter.
Bar / railshort / twice as long A bar is a short horizontal line, used at top, bottom or middle. A rail is about twice as long, and letters may sit or rest above it.
Other modifiers— Tilting, tumbling or toppling; walking, with legs and feet on the bottom; winged or flying; running, denoted by curves; dragged.

What is not in the grammar

Earlier versions of this sheet carried a table of notch widths, bar-face dimensions, minimum bend radii, clear-space minimums and a straight-run rule, presented as brand-design rules. None of that is on the page. It came from iron-making sources — how a smith builds the tool — and it was quietly promoted into rules about how the artwork should look.

Some of it is real and useful when the time comes to have an iron made: heat does pool in a sharp corner, smiths do file a notch at one, and lines set too close together do burn into each other. But it belongs to fabrication, not to the design, and putting it in the drawing produced three sheets of marks that no brand book would recognise.

The nine

Named modifier-first, as brands are read. Each on both real grounds, with its unit count and proportions.

Double M

2 units 1.72:1
Reads
Two letters, no modifier. Two units, which is the minimum a brand can be.
Note
The plainest thing that can be called a Double M, and the baseline everything below modifies.

Rocking Double M

3 units 1.44:1
Reads
Rocking double M. A curved mark attached to the bottom of the letters.
Grammar
Attachment is the whole distinction. The same curve drawn underneath but not touching is read “letter quarter circle” instead — a different brand with a different name.
Note
Closest of these to the approved Rocking Double Star: same modifier, letters where the stars were.
72 44 28

Swinging Double M

3 units 1.32:1
Reads
Swinging double M. A quarter or half circle attached to the top of a letter is swinging, as against rocking at the bottom.
Note
The mirror of the rocker, and the only modifier here that sits above the letters.
72 44 28

Bar Double M

3 units 1.32:1
Reads
Bar double M. A bar is a short horizontal line, usable at top, bottom or middle; the bar is named first because brands read top to bottom.
Note
One straight length of stock. The cheapest modifier there is.
72 44 28

Rail Double M

3 units 1.59:1
Reads
Rail double M. A rail is about twice as long as a bar, and letters may sit or rest above it.
Note
Does the rocker’s job of tying the pair together, with nothing curved to form.
72 44 28

Connected Double M

1 unit 1.50:1
Reads
Connected double M. The two M’s share their middle leg, and joined letters take “connected” in the reading.
Note
One unit and the simplest mark here — a single continuous run with nothing added to it.
72 44 28

Rocking Connected Double M

2 units 1.26:1
Reads
Rocking connected double M. Both modifiers, read in order: the curve, then the joined letters.
Note
Two units, and the most compact of the modified marks.

Lazy Double M

2 units 0.58:1
Reads
Lazy double M. Lazy means lying on its side.
Note
The only mark here taller than it is wide, which makes it the one to look at if a squarish lockup matters.

Tumbling Double M

2 units 1.08:1
Reads
Tumbling double M. Tilting, tumbling and toppling all describe a letter off its upright.
Note
The loosest of the nine, and the one that reads least like a wordmark.

Under the iron

Each mark as drawn, then with every stroke thickened 1.7× — the scar spreading as the iron meets hide. Kept because it is a fair check on whether a mark stays legible, not because it dictates the drawing.

Where they sort

Connected Double M is the only single-unit mark and the simplest thing here. Lazy Double M is the only one taller than wide, so it is the one to look at for a square slot. Rail and Bar tie the pair together with a straight length of stock rather than a formed curve. Rocking Double M keeps the modifier the approved star mark already uses.
